When did the fault move?

One fault on a depth section, checked before it goes into the fault framework: which intervals it grew through, which of those calls are larger than the pick uncertainty, and whether the reservoir faces its seal across the fault.

Maxim Balagurov, petroleum geologist. In my own work I interpret 3D seismic, build fault framework models and co-sign the exploration reports that rest on them. This page works through one check on a fault before it goes into the framework, from the depths of the same horizons on the two sides of a normal fault. The example below is made up.

Published work on building a geological model of a structurally complex area: Balagurov et al., 2026, Integrated Geophysical Interpretation for Geological Modeling of a Structurally Complex Region Influenced by Salt Tectonics and Magmatism (Eastern Siberia, Russia), Proceedings of IFEDC 2025, Springer. The example on this page is not taken from that paper.

AThe thicker side tells when the fault moved

An interval that is thicker on the downthrown side was laid down while the fault was moving. The extra thickness is the throw added in that time, provided sediment filled the space and nothing was eroded off the footwall. An interval of the same thickness on both sides shows no growth: any offset it has was added after it.

BSome of those calls are inside the pick uncertainty

CDoes the reservoir face its seal across the fault?

This is juxtaposition only: which beds lie against which at the fault. Whether the fault rock itself seals is a separate question, and so is the trap away from this section. A fault that moved after the seal was laid down has offset that seal, and the framework should say by how much. Whether it moved after charge is the sharper question, and needs the timing of charge.

    The numbers in full

    Limits

    • The numbers are made up. No survey, well or company data went into the example.
    • One section through the fault. Throw changes along strike. Repeat the check on several sections: throw should vary smoothly, and a sudden change that is not at a branch line or a relay is a pick or a correlation to check again.
    • Juxtaposition, and nothing about the fault rock. Smear, gouge and cementation can seal a reservoir-on-reservoir contact; fractures and later movement can make a sealing contact leak. The page does not model them.
    More limits
    • Normal faults only. A hanging wall that is thinner than the footwall is flagged, and may mean a wrong correlation across the fault, a fault that dies out downward, inversion, or strike-slip offset of a unit that changes thickness laterally.
    • Depths in metres after depth conversion. In two-way time the thickness ratios also carry the velocity change across the fault.
    • Thicknesses are vertical. Beds that dip 10 to 15° into the fault look 2 to 4 % thicker than they are, which is the size of a small expansion index.
    • Pick ± is taken as a hard limit, equally likely anywhere inside it and independent between picks. A pick on the wrong loop, one cycle out, is a different kind of error, 30 to 50 m, and is not covered. Near a fault the footwall pick is often the worse one.
    • A throw smaller than the data can resolve should be read as too close to call, whatever the arithmetic says.
    • Compaction and erosion are not restored. An interval thinned by erosion on the footwall looks like growth.
    • Each interval and each horizon is judged on its own. Neighbouring intervals share a pick, so their errors are linked; the page does not combine them.
    • The reservoir is read on the footwall side, a common trap against a normal fault. The hanging-wall side is not tested.
    How it works
    1. Throw at a horizon = its depth on the hanging wall minus its depth on the footwall.
    2. Thickness of an interval = depth of its base minus depth of its top, on each side. The difference, hanging wall minus footwall, is the growth; it is also the throw at the base minus the throw at the top. The expansion index is the ratio of the two thicknesses.
    3. Each pick may be off by anything within its ±, with equal chance. A throw rests on two picks and a difference in thickness on four, so their errors add. The chance that the true value is above zero is worked out exactly, from the distribution of a sum of such errors; nothing is drawn at random.
    4. The line on each bar is the range that holds the true difference eight times in ten, whatever level of sureness is chosen.
    5. The footwall reservoir is laid against the hanging wall at the same depths. Because the depths are cutoffs at the fault, the dip of the fault does not enter. The window above the seal is the part of the footwall reservoir that lies above the top of the seal on the hanging wall; its chance rests on two picks.
    Rules it uses
    • Thicker on the downthrown side means the fault was moving. The extra thickness is the throw added while that interval was deposited.
    • A difference in thickness rests on four picks. With the same ± on all four it has to exceed 1.0 times that ± to be resolved at 80 %, 1.5 times at 90 % and 1.9 times at 95 %.
    • The expansion index hides the uncertainty. A thin interval can show a large ratio from a difference of a few metres. Read the metres and their range first.
    • An offset horizon dates the last movement. The fault was still moving after the youngest horizon it clearly offsets, whether or not the interval above shows growth.
    • A fault that moved after the seal offsets the seal. Compare the throw at the top of the reservoir with the thickness of the seal on the hanging wall before calling the trap sealed.
