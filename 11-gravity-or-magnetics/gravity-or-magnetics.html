Gravity, magnetics or both?

A dolerite sill sits in a sedimentary basin, and seismic shows its top. Before a survey is ordered, the page tells you what gravity and magnetics will each see from the ground, a UAV or an aircraft; what one field alone cannot tell apart; and which pair of field and data pins the thickness.

Why it matters in petroleum work: the sills of trap provinces lie inside sedimentary basins. Their strong top reflection is easy to pick on seismic; the base, and so the thickness, often is not. Thickness changes the velocity model, the depth of every horizon below, and the drilling plan through hard rock. Gravity and magnetics see the sill from different sides.

1. What each field sees

Field and platformHeight, mPeakNoise÷ noiseVerdict

Ground gravity: about 0.03 mGal when station heights come from GNSS to ±10 cm (Seigel, 1995). Moving-platform gravity is far noisier: one airborne system quotes 0.2 mGal at 2.2 to 4 km resolution from fixed-wing aircraft (Sander Geophysics); strapdown systems on aircraft and UAVs report sub-mGal up to 2 mGal (Vyazmin and Golovan, 2023). The UAV preset, 2 mGal at 1 km, is my assumption for a small, low, slow UAV: a quadcopter reached 2–3 mGal RMS along repeated lines (Dale et al., 2026; resolution not given in the abstract), and a large helicopter-type UAV at 25 m/s, flown at 340–420 m above the ellipsoid, gave a repeat-line scatter of 0.61 mGal at 1.25 km (Vyazmin and Golovan, 2023). Magnetic presets follow tool 02 and are my assumption, not from a source. For comparison: a noise envelope of about ±0.05 nT in one fixed-wing UAV survey (reviewed by Zheng et al., 2021), and noise limits of < 1 to < 10 nT set in earlier UAV studies (listed by Tuck et al., 2021).

How it works

  • The sill is a horizontal slab, long along strike, with a flat top and base. Gravity is the textbook closed form for a 2D rectangle (Blakely, 1995). Over the middle of a wide sill it tends to the Bouguer slab, 2πGΔρt: 0.042 mGal per metre of sill per g/cm³ of contrast.
  • Magnetics uses the same slab in a vertical field with induced magnetisation, as in tool 02: k F / 2π times the angle the top subtends at the sensor minus the angle the base subtends. Over a sill many times wider than its depth the two cancel in the middle and the signal sits at the edges; the width of the edge anomaly carries the depth.
  • Moving-platform gravity is filtered. The page smooths the profile with a Gaussian filter that keeps half the amplitude at twice the resolution length, and counts one independent reading per resolution length or reading spacing, whichever is longer.
  • Which pair pins it: a linearised least-squares analysis with six unknowns (top, thickness, density contrast, susceptibility contrast, two edges), the readings with their noise, and what is known beforehand. The table shows how far each unknown can still move.

Rules it uses

  • Visible: peak at least 5 × the noise of one reading is clear, 2 to 5 × marginal, under 2 × lost, as in tool 02. Gravity peak: over the middle of the sill. Magnetic peak: the largest value along the line; the low beyond the edge adds to the swing.
  • Same data: two sills whose profiles differ by less than 2 × the noise of one reading are counted as the same data on one reading; table 3 shows what the whole line of readings can still tell. Tests: thickness × 1.5 with the contrast ÷ 1.5; top 20 % deeper with the contrast refitted by least squares.
  • Before the data: top ±25 % of its depth; thickness, density contrast and susceptibility ±50 %; edges ±1 km. Seismic replaces the top with its own ± m. A well replaces the density contrast and the susceptibility with its own ± %. Its top and thickness hold only at the well, so the page does not carry them across the sill.
  • Readings: gravity one per reading spacing or resolution length, whichever is longer; magnetics one per 100 m. Profile: the sill and five times its depth on each side.
  • The thresholds 5 × and 2 ×, the tests and the ±15 % and ±30 % bands are my conventions.

Limits

  • A 2D sill with flat top and base, uniform rock, flat ground. Real sills step between levels, thin out and have feeders; the page shows the trade-offs, not a model of a real sill.
  • Vertical field and induced magnetisation only, which suits high magnetic latitudes. Dolerites often carry strong remanence, which can change the sign of the magnetic anomaly; core measurements tell.
  • The magnetic noise is instrument and survey noise only. In trap provinces, shallow lavas, tuffs and other sills add geological magnetic noise, which can be much larger. The UAV itself makes interference near the sensor, with peaks of 21.4 to 574.2 nT on four mapped UAVs (Tuck et al., 2021); the sensor is moved away from it or the interference is compensated.
  • The regional field and other density contrasts (salt, basement) are not modelled. In practice the sill is separated from a regional trend, which adds its own error; the noise figures do not include it.
  • Noise is independent between readings. Levelling errors and other correlated noise make things worse.
  • Linearised: the spreads hold for small changes around the sill you describe. A real design and inversion run in 3D with all wells and horizons. The page frames the question; it does not replace the operator's design.

Stage: proposed programme. The example is made up and the page reports no survey. In a project, the geologist sets the geological question and interprets the data; a licensed operator designs and runs the survey. The check builds on the author's experience integrating potential-field and field data in Arctic and East Siberian regional projects; UAV acquisition is the proposed extension.
