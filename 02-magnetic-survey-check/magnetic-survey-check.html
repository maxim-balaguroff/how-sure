What must the survey resolve?

A magnetic survey should be specified by the geological question, not by habit. Describe the body you need to see and the survey you have in mind. The page tells you whether the body will stand above the noise, whether two bodies will show as two, and how far apart the lines can be.

Why it matters in petroleum work: where igneous intrusions cut a sedimentary basin, dolerite sills and dykes blur the seismic image, can break up reservoirs and seals, and cause trouble while drilling. Magnetic data map them cheaply, before the wells are placed.

How it works

  • For a body that runs a long way down, magnetised by a vertical field, the anomaly is proportional to the angle its top subtends at the sensor (for a plug, the solid angle; for a sill edge, the angle of its top minus the angle of its base). The page sums exactly that.
  • Depth and sensor height add up. What counts is the distance from the sensor to the top of the body, h.
  • Flying lower helps a shallow body a lot and a deep one very little. The table above shows how much.

Rules it uses

  • Visible: peak at least 5 × noise is clear, 2 to 5 × is marginal, under 2 × is lost. These thresholds are my convention; if your project uses others, read the table against them. For a sill edge the verdict uses the high on one side; the swing to the low beyond the edge is twice that, so the verdict errs on the safe side.
  • Two bodies: on a noise-free total-field profile that crosses both, two thin bodies only show two peaks when they are more than about 1.15 h apart; two small plugs, more than about h. A dip smaller than twice the noise is not counted. A vertical-derivative map can split them a little closer if the noise allows.
  • Lines: a plug midway between lines spaced h apart keeps about 72 % of its peak; at 2 h, about 35 %. The 70 % rule for the spacing is mine. Reid (1980): up to about 2 h for a smooth total-field grid, about h for gradients, h/2 for a single point-like target.
  • Long bodies: lines within about 45° of perpendicular to strike cross a dyke longer than a few line spacings on every line; the spacing then decides how well the gridded map follows it (beyond about 2 h it breaks into beads), not whether it is seen. Oblique crossings widen the anomaly by 1/sin of the angle.

Limits

  • Vertical field and induced magnetisation only. Outside high latitudes the raw anomaly of a long body is smaller by about sin²I + cos²I·sin²β (I inclination, β strike from magnetic north): 0.4 for a north-striking dyke at I = 40°, 0.03 at I = 10°. Reduction to the pole restores the shape but amplifies the noise by the same factor, so read the verdict as optimistic by that factor. No remanence; dolerites often carry it, and it can change the sign.
  • Idealised bodies running to infinite depth: a dyke infinitely long, a straight sill edge, a plug as a vertical cylinder. A thin dyke whose base is b below the sensor gives about (1 − h/b) of the value shown, a thin plug about (1 − h²/b²); bodies wider than they are deep are overstated. Flat ground, constant sensor height.
  • Noise is one number: diurnal, heading and levelling errors and the geological and cultural background are assumed to be inside it. Ground readings are treated as continuous along the line; the station spacing is not checked. The page frames the question for a survey design; it does not replace the contractor's design.
